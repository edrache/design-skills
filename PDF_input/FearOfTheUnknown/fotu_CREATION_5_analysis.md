## Rules Analysis

Let's  take  a  look  at  the  rules  in  a  bit  more  depth.  This section doesn't add any new rules, it just supplies commentary on them, so you can understand the rules as intennded a bit better, as well as the motivations for why they are the way they are. The information here should help you grok the reasoning behind the current state of the rules, so you'll have an easier time if you want to hack them (see page 146) and make it easier for you to run or play in sessions of Fear of the Unknown.

## When to Make a Move

The  moves  are  intentionally  named  as  short  phrases. Whenever you describe what your character is doing and your description uses (or could use) one of those phrases, that's a clue that you should probably use that move. The Oracle is the one who decides if or when to invoke a move. If you think a certain move would be appropriate, however, then by all means suggest it to the Oracle.

You  should Investigate  whenever  you  have  a  question you're trying to answer , especially if you can't answer it in the current scene. The Investigate move creates a new scene where you can find some information you're looking for.

You need to Face Peril whenever you're in a situation that could plausibly lead to your death . It likely won't, if you have any of the 7-9 options left, but you'll still need to roll for it - and get ever closer to death in the process.

You need to Encounter True Horror whenever you're in a  particularly  horrifying  situation ,  especially  if  it's  your  first time dealing with that kind of thing. You'll probably have to do it every time you get attacked, the first time you see a dead body, every time you see a particularly gruesomely killed dead body, encounter an unearthly creature, learn something man was not meant to know, see actual magic, etc.

You can make any of the revelations whenever you want to reveal something about yourself or the world and you have enough of the relevant kind of experience (Horror, Humanity, or Foreboding). This could be on a relatively quiet day in between killings,  or  on  the  drive  to  the  final  showdown,  or  during  the end credits after you've seemingly killed the maniac, or while downing  an  entire  bottle  of  whiskey  after  narrowly  escaping death, or at any other time. Revelations can represent revealing previously  unmentioned  backstory. They  can  be  done  in  a flashback, a montage, or any other short scene that you describe.

## Assisting Someone

In  order  to  assist  someone  on  Investigate  or  Face  Peril, you need to have an appropriate positive or wild tag you can invoke. Because there is a maximum of 3 positive or wild tags that anyone can invoke on a single roll, this means that at most 3 protagonists can cooperate on a single check (or 4, if the lead investigator doesn't contribute one). But what if you have a party of more players than can contribute? Well, then you'd better split up to look for clues! You'll be able to cover more ground that way.

That may strike you as boneheaded and dangerous, but it  is  intentional. This game is meant to emulate horror movies, after all, so the mechanics incentivize acting like a character in a horror movie. And characters in horror movies split up like that all  the time. If the players do split up to search for clues more efficiently, you as the Oracle have the responsibility of not letting one group get bored while you handle the other group. One good way to do that is to start resolving one group's move, then when you get to a point where the tension is high and you're about to reveal important information, cut to the other group. Then when they're at a high tension moment, switch back to the first group and resolve their scene, then cut back to the second group and resolve their scene. Think of how a movie will cut back and forth between multiple bits of action in this form, as a way of raising tension and keeping the audience's interest.

## Investigate

Now that we've made the town and our characters, let's get into the actual rules of play. Our first move, Investigate, is our bread and butter. It's the move that players will make the most often, and it will create the majority of the scenes in any given session of Fear of the Unknown.

The first thing to understand is the purpose of this move. It is twofold: 1) to create a new scene, and 2) to reveal information to  the  players.  Number  2  is  hopefully  fairly  obvious:  making an  Investigate  check  starts  by  the  investigating  player  asking a  question,  and  then  most  results  of  making  the  Investigate move give the investigating player a clue, a piece of information about the question they're asking. In fact, most results - and all successful (7+) results if the player is being assisted - give more than one clue, allowing for followup questions.

The other aspect of the Investigate move is creating scenes. While many RPGs are continuous in their narrative - in order to get from location A to location B, you need to roleplay that bit of travel - Fear of the Unknown is more discrete, a series of connected scenes. You can have a scene in location A, and then if there's nothing that would stop you from getting to location B, you can have the next scene in location B, with no need to play out the travel in between if nothing interesting would happen during it.

Here's how the Investigate move creates a scene. First, let's dive into the mechanics. There are several important factors here.

1.  the player asks a question they want their character to learn the anwer to, which starts the mechanics in motion, then
2.  they choose which positive or wild tags to invoke (including any from other players assisting), up to a maximum of 3, then
3. the  Oracle  picks  what  negative  or  wild  tags  they  wish  to invoke, up to a maximum of 2, then
4.  the player rolls two 6 sided dice, adds the number of tags they chose, subtracts the number of tags the Oracle chose, and sees

- what range the result lies in, and finally
5.  if the result is a 7+ the player chooses one option, and if the result is 6- the Oracle chooses one or more options.

Once the player has finished that  process,  we  move  out of the mechanics and back into the fiction. We use the tags and results from the mechanics to create a scene that that player, the Oracle, and any other involved players, then act out. The outcome of this scene needs to line up with the outcome prescribed by the mechanics, and incorporate the tags that the player(s) invoked (it's okay to ignore the tags the Oracle invoked, unless the player got a total of 6-, in which case they should come into play).

## Building a scene

Let's take a look at how the same mechanical result, but with  different  tags  invoked,  could  create  two  very  different scenes. Suppose the players have stumbled upon a dead body, which appears to have been poisoned. Two players both want to get answers to the question 'where could this poison have been acquired?'

One player says 'I'm gonna go talk to my mob connections and see if they know anything. If any of them are reluctant to talk, I'll try to scare them into talking with my intimidating physique .' The  Oracle  says  'Okay.  I'm  going  to  invoke  your made  some enemies tag .  Some of them might not take kindly to you asking these sorts of questions.'

He gains a humanity from having a negative tag invoked, rolls a 7+2-1=8, and gets an answer, and gets to ask a followup question. The Oracle says 'a lanky man who looks older than he is, with dark circles under his eyes, listens to your description of the dead body. 'Yeah, that sounds like datura poisoning. It's a kind of nightshade. I haven't sold anybody that any time recently, and I'm the only person you'll find selling it in town. Somebody must  have  their  own  supply'  the  tired  man  says.'  The  player asks his followup question: 'Who in town might be able to grow this?' The Oracle replies 'The thin man leans back and looks at the ceiling, thinking. 'There's no way that the legit flower shops would sell this. And it can't grow wild in this climate. My guess is  you're  best  off  talking  to  some  weirdo  with  a  fancy  private greenhouse. Maybe the botany professor at the university?''

Another player says 'I'm going to ask the other old ladies at my gardening club if any of them know what kind of plant this poison might be from, and if so, where someone in town might have acquired it. I'll also invoke my town gossip wild tag, in case I'm  able  to  hear  any  other  juicy  rumours  around  town'.  The Oracle adds 'Okay, though I'm also going to invoke your refuses to use modern technology tag - it'll take you a while to go around town on foot getting these answers, so there's always a chance something bad might happen while you're doing that.'

She gains a humanity from having a negative tag invoked and rolls a 7+2-1=9, and gets an answer, and gets to ask a follow up question. The Oracle says 'your friend Gertrude listens to your description of the dead man's body while sipping her tea, then sets her cup down. 'That does sound familiar. In the old country, we called that plant the Devil's Trumpet. I knew a woman who killed  her  good  for  nothing  husband in just the same manner. A bit of the petals mixed in with his tea, and down he went,' she  tells  you.'  The  player  replies  'Thank  you,  Gertie.  Do  you have any idea where the killer might have gotten the plant?' The Oracle answers 'The old woman pours herself more tea. 'Well, whoever they are, they didn't get it at my flower shop, that's for sure. Nor did they find it in the wild - it's much too cold here for them to grow. I know of only two people who have a greenhouse that could have grown it. Mr. Witherbee might, but wait, no - he focuses on jungle plants, and Devil's Trumpet would die in that kind of wet. No, if I were you I'd go ask Professor Jimson at the university if he's sold any, or had any stolen. I don't know for sure that he's the one who grew it, but the hot, dry greenhouse they have up on the hill is the only environment around here that I can think of where it would grow.' She dissolves a sugar cube in her tea and changes the topic to more mundane town gossip.'

Here  we  see  two  very  different  scenes  where  two  very different  characters  learn  the  same  information. This  is  the purpose  of  the  Investigate  move:  for  players  to  craft  scenes that involve the specific traits of their character. Stories are as much about characters as they are about what happens, and this is how the mechanics support the differences between different characters mattering in terms of what scenes make up the story.

## The Investigate Results

Now  let's  look  at  some  specific  options  and  how  the players (and Oracle) might use them. First up, the 10+ options. It's  importat  to  note  that  for  both  the  10+  and  7-9  options  in Investigate, unlike the options for any of the other moves, you do not have to pick one - you could choose to leave the ones you have to mark off for later, or not pick the recovery option just yet, because you're not willing to pay the cost just yet.

## 10+

- O You find something useful. You gain a wild tag, such as a shotgun , or blackmail on that obnoxious dilettante , or a fragment of an Elder Sign . Mark this off.

This option doesn't allow for a follow-up question (unless someone is assisting)  but  instead  grants  a  permanent  ongoing bonus in the form of a new positive tag. Whatever tag you pick will be a permanent (unless you lose it as part of another move, the  same  as  you  might  any  other  positive  tag)  part  of  your character.  This  can  represent  character  growth,  gaining  a  new resource, gaining a new ally, or anything else. It's similar to the Take Care of Yourself revelation. It's important to note that, just like any other situation where your character gains a positive tag, this can also represent revealing a new aspect of your character's backstory. You have to mark this option off.

- O You find an additional clue. Ask a follow up question, or a question about something else. Mark this off.

You gain another clue, which can be about anything - it can be a followup question, or it could represent your character stumbling upon information that's not relevant to the question at hand, but is still useful for solving the mystery. You have to mark this option off, too.

- Someone notices your investigation, and takes an interest. Recover the other two options.

This option allows you to recover the other two options. However, it adds a fictional twist that you'll have to deal with: whoever is  interested  in  your  investigation.  This  could  be  the killer noticing you, or a helpful neighborhood kid who becomes curious  and  starts  following  you  around,  or  anyone  else.  It could be helpful, or harmful, or both. It does not have a direct mechanical effect, but should come into play in the story, either immediately or later on, and when it does at least one move is likely to be used.

## 7-9

Now let's look at the 7-9 options. Again, you don't have to pick one of these, though you likely will want to.

- O You  find  an  additional  clue,  relevant  to  the  question  you were asking. Ask a follow up question, then mark this off.

This is  fairly  straightforward:  you  get  to  ask  a  followup question  after  receiving  your  first  clue.  This  could  represent continuing  to  interrogate  a  witness,  getting  an  anonymous phonecall with more information, finding another physical clue at a crime scene, or anything else that gives you more information. You have to mark this option off.

O You find something useful during your investigation. You gain a temporary tag, like the killer's weapon , or keys to... something , or the cultist's book . Mark this off

A more temporary version of the 10+ result, this gives you something you can use to continue your investigation or aid you when you need to Face Peril against the antagonist. Of course, since this is a temporary tag, you never know when you'll lose out on it, and it will most likely be at the worst possible moment. Of course, only a truly cruel Oracle wouldn't let you use it to your advantage at least once.

- While  you're  investigating,  something  significant  happens elsewhere. Recover the other two options.

This option allows you to recover the other two options. It represents something happening 'off screen'. Perhaps another murder  happens,  which  the  Oracle  describes  in  a  giallo  style 'black glove killer' scene, where we the audience (the players, not the characters) see the murder without seeing any revealing details  about  the  killer,  or  perhaps  the  person  who  took  an interest in your investigation when you picked the 10+ recovery option reports your activities to the police, or anything else that doesn't directly involve the protagonists and happens from the audience's perspective.

That  rounds  out  the  7+  options,  which  are  the  options where the player definitely gets a clue and the player is the one who picks the option. Now on to the 6- options, where the player might  get  a  clue,  but  the  Oracle  is  the  one  who  gets  to  pick. Another important difference here is that the Oracle gets to pick one or more of the 6- options, whereas the player would only get to pick one option on a 7+.

The most important thing to remember, as the Oracle, is that the fictional positioning should always change after someone makes a move. You should never be in a situation where someone tries something and fails and then just tries again. Things should always  change  enough  that  they  have  to  try  something  new. Maybe they still have the same question in mind, but need to pick a new set of tags to investigate it - that way there's a whole new scene, rather than just rehashing the current one.

- Your negative tags that were invoked come into play in a major way.

This is the most important and basic option. The Oracle should almost always pick this, either on its own or in addition to the other options below. This is a primarily fictional response -  it  doesn't  necessarily  pull  in  any  other  mechanics  (though  it certainly can - Face Peril if the tag presents a physical danger, or  Encounter  True  Horror  if  it  represents  a  mental  danger,  or perhaps a temporary tag, or something else). If you invoked any negative tags, you should pick this option, so that they have an effect on the story. If you didn't want them to affect the story, then why would you invoke them?

- You can still  discover  something,  but  something  else  goes wrong

Fairly  straightforward:  the  protagonist  still  finds  some useful information, but something else goes wrong. This could be them getting into danger, it could be the killer striking elsewhere, it  could  be  them  having to make a bargain of some kind. You should pick this one pretty often.

- You gain a temporary tag - work with the Oracle to determine what it is.

This option is a way of making any of the other options have  more  mechanical  weight.  Perhaps  you  learn  what  you wanted to know but now you owe someone a favor, or perhaps your car breaks down on the way back into town, or perhaps you pissed off the sheriff by not backing down from the investigation like he told you to. Whatever it is, it'll go away at some point, but until then it will occasionally impede you - and maybe even help you!

- You learn a horrible secret that tests your mettle, and have to Encounter True Horror.

This  could  represent  a  multitude  of  possible  fictional scenarios,  from  reading  from  a  forbidden  tome  to  discovering that someone the protagonist loved and trusted is a victim (or the  antagonist!).  It  could  include  useful  information  or  not, depending on the situation and what is learned.

- Someone else learns something about you that you'd rather they didn't.

This option draws in an NPC. This could extend the current scene, or set up something for later on. You don't always have to reveal that this has happened right away (though you typically should - remember, you can reveal things to the players without revealing them to their characters), as long as you reveal it later on.

- You end up in a treacherous situation, and likely have to Face Peril.

Investigations  can  go  poorly!  Perhaps  the  protagonists have  attracted  unwanted  attention  and  they  want  to  get  you to  stop  asking  questions,  or  perhaps they've fallen afoul of an environmental danger while snooping around, or perhaps they stumble upon the killer and have to immediately confront them. This  option  ups  the  danger  and  forces  the  plot  forward  in  a dramatic way. An excellent choice if player attention is flagging or you're running short on time.

- Someone who assisted you suffers in some way

This  option  draws  in  anyone  who  assisted  the  lead protagonist. This is especially relevant if you invoked a negative or wild tag that the assisting protagonist has, or their bond.

- Something bad happens to or at a town tag that was invoked.

Fear of the Unknown is a game about setting as well as people. The location changes just as much as the characters do, and this  is  one  of  the  ways  it  can.  This  could  be  as  bad  as  to mark off the town tag, if it gets thoroughly destroyed (the mine collapses, the killer offs the mayor, the biker bar gets shut down by the authorities), or just something bad that happens. Which leads into…

- Something significant happens elsewhere.

The same as the 7-9 option, only it doesn't let them recover anything. A good way to build up tension, especially early in an investigation.

- An NPC in the scene gets in some sort of trouble.

The  protagonists  aren't  the  only  ones  you  can  threaten! This lets you build up the tension without directly hurting the protagonists, letting you keep that card in reserve. The players often expect their protagonists to be in danger, and can be even more concerned when their NPC friends get in trouble.

- Something bad happens elsewhere while you're distracted.

The antagonist isn't just going to wait for the protagonists. They're still doing things, advancing their agenda, offing victims, moving their plans forward, while the protagonists are trying to figure out what they're up to.

- Something goes very wrong, and you have to mark off a tag.

This is a very bad thing. Picking this option is a big deal, so  don't  do  it  lightly.  It  could  represent  an  NPC  they  have  a connection with getting offed, or a valuable resource or ability being lost. Using this too often or too early can be dispiriting to players,  especially  new  ones,  so  be  careful  with  this  option.  It should have a big impact every time you pick it. Keep in mind that the player picks which tag they mark off, though you can make a suggestion.

- Some other devious scenario of the Oracle's invention.

A catch all for anything else you come up with that isn't on this list. The other options aren't meant as an exhaustive list of things that you can choose, just as suggestions - though you should be picking from them more often than not.

## Face Peril

Face  Peril  has  a  lot  in  common  with  Investigate,  and  of course is also very different in many ways. While it absolutely can be used to create scenes - especially climactic confrontations with the antagonist - it can also be used in the middle of other scenes, if a serious danger suddenly arises. The results of other moves can lead into Face Peril, and it can happen on its own, just as a result of roleplaying.

The trigger to remember is that if someone is in a situation where they could plausibly die, whatever it is, then they need to Face Peril.

## The Face Peril Results

Now let's take a look at the specific options, starting with the 10+ ones. Note that unlike with Investigate, you must pick an option - you can't pass on it.

## 10+

- O Your experience, and your success at it, affects you for the better. You gain a positive tag, such as a sense of calm ,  or a sharp eye ,  or  even (with the Oracle's approval) I  know what hurts them . Mark this off.

One of the best possible results, and setting up the pattern for 10+ results on this move. Gaining a positive tag which represents an  improvement  to  you  as  a  person.  Horror  film  protagonists often, if they are particularly lucky, become more capable after their first confrontation with the killer. Think Sidney Prescott in the Scream series.

- O You find something that gets you out of the situation safely. You gain a positive tag, such as an old camp axe , or a cultist's decoder ring , or a truck . Mark this off.

Continuing the pattern, this represents finding a physical object that helps you out. Think Laurie Strode picking up Michael Myers' knife in Halloween.

- O You make or reveal a connection with an NPC who helps you  out.  Gain  a  wild  tag  naming  them,  such  as my  study partner Szandor , or my childhood bestie Rebecca , or 'Laces' the hobo . Mark this off.

The last in this pattern, this either pulls in an existing NPC or  creates  a  new  one  (giving  the  Oracle  more  to  play  around with!). Think Dick Hallorann in The Shining (especially the book).

- You succeed, but barely, at the skin of your teeth. It costs you something. Mark off a positive or wild tag, or a town tag that was invoked on this roll. Recover one of the other three options.

And now we see the payoff to this pattern. This option only recovers one of the other options, not all three. And it costs you a positive tag (or a town tag!) to do so. Thus we get a character who's up 3 positive tags from the above options, then loses one, and gains a new one, loses one, gains one, et cetera. This means that while your character has infinite potential for change from this move, they have only a finite potential for improvement from it.

The option to instead destroy a town tag lets us tell stories about characters in a society that's collapsing around them, while they could even be growing stronger in the process.

## 7-9

The 7-9 options replace the idea of 'hit points' that you see in many games. Instead of a simple numerical value that counts down, which can often be healed, and which is different from character  to  character,  we  instead  give  all  characters  the  same number  of  possible  options,  and  then  their  player  picks  tags that make the most sense for the character and their situation. Whenever you pick  a  7-9  option  you  grow  one  step  closer  to death, though you do always have a chance to survive any given time you Face Peril, as long as you roll a 10+ and have tags left to sacrifice to save yourself.

O Suffer a minor injury - lose a temporary tag you invoked in this scene, or gain a temporary tag, like I smacked my hand , or I'm feeling dizzy , or I lost my glasses . Mark this off.

Starting with the least horrific option, this choice actually has two possibilities in it: first, you could lose a temporary tag, or second, you could gain a temporary tag. Losing a temporary tag is something like 'I'll say that the shotgun I bought at the gun store on our way to the camp has run out of shells. I shout out that it's empty and toss it aside'. The important thing here is that you can only sacrifice a temporary tag that you invoked, not the Oracle.

The other option is gaining a temporary tag that represents a minor injury. This won't come into play many times, and will go away on its own. It's minor because it doesn't last long - likely the rest of this scene, and then perhaps one or two more. It should come into play at least once, if at all possible.

O Suffer a chronic injury - gain a negative tag, like horrific scar , or missing an ea r, or permanent limp . Mark this off.

Now we're starting to get into more serious injury territory. This gives you a permanent negative tag - unlike the minor injury, it  won't  resolve  on  its  own,  but  instead  becomes  a  permanent part of your character moving forward.

O Suffer  a  crippling  injury  -  gain  a  negative  tag,  like broken leg , or missing eye , or lost a hand . If you don't receive medical attention soon, choose an additional result on this list. If you do, replace the negative tag with a wild tag, like uses a sturdy silver-topped cane ,  or wears a mesmerizing glass eye ,  or has an artificial hand . Mark this off.

One  of  the  scariest  options  to  pick,  this  one  seriously changes the fictional positioning. Suddenly there's a ticking clock that you need to attend to, with serious risk if you don't, and also significant reward if you do.

If  you do get medical attention, while you don't get this option  back,  you  do  mark  off  the  negative  tag,  and  you  gain a wild tag - a pretty nice reward! Most often in the form of an assistive device, this can be a nice bonus going forward, though it will also occasionally get in your way (it is a wild tag, after all) - typically by being denied it. After all, a cane isn't useful if it gets knocked out of your hand.

If you don't get medical attention, though, that's when this gets dangerous. Not only do you not get the wild tag, not only do you have to keep the negative tag, but you also have to pick another option off this list - potentially dying, and if not, moving even closer to death.

- You  manage  to  escape  unscathed,  but  something  in  town doesn't. Mark off a town tag you or the Oracle invoked in this scene.

Another  way  that  the  town  evolves  as  you  play.  This represents part of the town being destroyed in the conflict. This could be entirely accidental - the flood destroys the library - or intentional on your part - you run your 18 wheeler right through the hippie compound to avoid having to slow down - or it could be intentional on the part of the antagonist - they may not have caught you, but they'll kill your beloved anthropology professor right in front of you.

This may seem like it lets the players off the hook in terms of moving closer to death, since this option can be chosen over and over, but remember that there are a finite number of town tags  to  mark  off,  and  that  this  will  significantly  change  the setting and story every time it's chosen, and that sort of change is really what's most important. This isn't a game of 'Oracle vs Protagonists', this is a game of collaborative storytelling.

Remember as well that the tag should be invoked in order for you to be able to pick it with this option. The tags build up the scene, so no fair pulling in ones that aren't involved in the scene. If  the  tag  can  be  affected  by  the  scene,  then  you  or  the  Oracle should invoke it before you roll.

O You die. Work with the Oracle to describe how you die.

Fairly straightforward! Typically but not always  the option of last resort, this one kills you. You may die suddenly and  shockingly  abruptly,  or  you  may  have  time  to  finish  the scene before bleeding out, or this option could even represent your character being arrested, charged, sentenced, and executed. Work with the Oracle to come up with something that makes sense in the fiction and is dramatically satisfying.

Something  happened  one  time  in  a  game  that  I  loved, which  was  that  during  the  Epilogue  move,  a  player  used  the Reveal Something About Yourself move to recover this option on Face Peril, and picked this option, revealing that their character was alive during the credits. I love this. Please encourage that sort of idea in your players.

6-

Similar guidance to the Oracle here as in the Investigate move. Unlike the players, the Oracle can pick one or more options, though they should usually pick at least the first option, and the fictional positioning should always change whenever a move is made. The result should never be 'you fail, try again'. Especially with Face Peril, there should be major consequences to failure though they may not happen to the character who tried it!

Remember that the protagonist (and anyone who assisted them) gains a foreboding whenever they roll a 6-. This means there's  always  something  interesting  and  rewarding  for  the players going on, no matter what they roll.

- Your negative tags that were invoked come into play in a major way.

The same as with Investigate, this option should almost always come into play on a 6- roll. This is how the negative tags affect  the  story  and  current  situation.  The  Oracle  should  only invoke negative tags that they want to see come into play, so they should almost always pick this option! Don't be afraid to pick this and one or more other options as well: the negative tags are often what will cause the other fictional or mechanical effects to happen.

- You die. This should only happen in particularly dangerous or foolhardy situations or in the climax.

The second way to die, the other being picking the final option on 7-9. This can also be fun to invoke during a climactic encounter, especially if this is a one-shot where the player isn't going  to  use  their  character  again,  like  at  a  convention.  The protagonist should go out in a suitably dramatic way here - going down in a blaze of glory, suddenly dying when they thought they were safe as the killer gets up again after everyone was sure they were dead, or dying in a particularly awesome and gruesome way.

- You mark off one or more 7-9 options.

A good way to make a 6- at least as punishing as a 7-9. A good basis if you're unsure what else to do for a selection of 6moves is to pick this option and have their negative tags come into play in a way that really changes up the fictional positioning to make things worse for them.

- Other protagonists in the scene need to mark off one or more 7-9 options.

Pretty  straightforward.  You  probably  shouldn't  do  this super  often,  but  it's  a  fine  way  to  ratchet  up  the  danger  and tension, especially if the other protagonists thought they were safe or it's the climactic confrontation.

- You find yourself in a yet more dangerous position.

The scene changes, for the worse. They'll have to Face Peril or Encounter True Horror in order to escape, and have to deal with whatever other options you've picked in addition to this one on this roll.

- Something truly horrible happens, and you have to Encounter True Horror.

In addition to whatever else is going on, they're horrified by it. The mechanics are straightforward on this: when you're in a horrific situation, you Encounter True Horror.

- Something terrible happens to someone who assisted you.

Here's the risky part to assisting someone! It's not just a free +1, they're also exposing themself to this danger. This could be that they have to Face Peril, or they have to Encounter True Horror, or are subject to another of these options.

- Something bad happens to a town tag that was invoked.

Fear  of  the  Unknown  is  a  game  about  setting  as  well as  characters,  and  you  should  always  have  in  your  mind that whatever is going on is affecting the town as well as the protagonists. This could be marking off a town tag, representing its total destruction, or another murder happening there while they're dealing with this peril, or merely tarnishing its reputation, or anything else that negatively affects it.

- An NPC in the scene gets hurt, or worse.

This is a horror game. It's not very fun if the protagonists die  immediately  from  their  first  bad  roll,  but  people  should probably die. The solution? Kill off NPCs. This could be a random stranger who just got unlucky, or it could be an important NPC that the protagonist has a strong connection to. It can be a sudden, shocking death, or it could be a slow, drawn out torturous death. Get creative with it! Fun kills are a major draw in horror movies, and this is your chance to inflict those on someone without removing any  protagonists from the game.

- Something bad happens elsewhere while you're distracted.

This is an important one. The protagonists aren't going to be where all of the action is all of the time, so it's very useful to have something significant happen somewhere they aren't. It puts pressure on them and makes the threat to the town feel real. You can reveal this to the players immediately by describing a scene that the protagonists don't see, or wait for them to discover it.

- Something goes very wrong, and you have to mark off a tag.

Picking this  option  is  a  pretty  big  deal.  It  can  be  paired with an NPC that the tag represents dying, or it could represent a loss of an item, or an ability, or all sorts of problems. This can really harm a player's concept for their character, so be careful picking this one. Remember that the player gets to choose which tag they

- Some other devious scenario of the Oracle's invention.

The same as on Investigate - a catch all for anything else you come up with that isn't on this list. The other options aren't meant as an exhaustive list of things that you can choose, just as suggestions - though you should be picking from them more often than not.

## Encounter True Horror

You might be used to playing other games that address the questions of seeing and experiencing things that horrify or traumatize you, where you start with a set amount of 'sanity' that goes down as you play, and as it goes down you become more and more 'insane' until you 'go mad', often rolling for different mental illnesses (perhaps called 'madnesses' or 'insanities') on a random table.

Fear of the Unknown tracks that idea a bit differently. First, you don't have a set amount of 'sanity' that you start with and goes down. Instead, you start with zero horror, and every time you experience something traumatic, you gain a horror (and first, make this move).

The result of rolling on the move and the option you pick, represents  your  immediate  reaction  to  whatever  is  horrifying you. Gaining one horror going forward represents your character becoming  more  shaken  and  upset  and  traumatized  as  they continue to experience horrifying events. That horror makes it more and more likely that  worse  (higher)  results  will  happen as the story goes on, and you can also get rid of it with the Let the Horror Sink In revelation, which represents your long term ability to cope with the horror.

One of  the  important  safety  mechanics  which  is  always built into the game is that you never roll randomly for a trauma symptom, and the Oracle never picks your negative tags - you, the  player,  always  do .  The  negative  tags  will  have  the  same mechanical effect, of course, but this means that you will never be in the position of having to roleplay a symptom you have to deal with in real life and want to escape in the game, or dealing with  something  you  don't  think  you  can  accurately  represent and don't want to try, or even just having a reaction that doesn't make sense in the fiction.

You should also check in with the other players (ideally when  setting  up  lines  and  veils,  see  page 15)  if  you're  going to make a serious mental illness part of how you roleplay your character, and do your best to roleplay it respectfully.

All  players  should  be  ready  for  all  of  the  characters  to experience trauma and not necessarily react to it in the healthiest ways - this is a horror game, after all - but that doesn't mean you have to have everything goes in every game. For instance, maybe one player's brother is schizophrenic, and she wants extra care to be taken that that's not played as a 'wacky' or comedic illness. Perhaps another player used to self harm by burning himself with cigarettes, and that particular form of violence would really set them off, but a gruesome mutilation wouldn't. Perhaps someone is a recovering anorexic or alcoholic and doesn't want to have to deal with that in game as well as out.

## The Encounter True Horror Results

Encounter True Horror has one more results range than either  Investigate  or  Face  Peril.  They  all  have  6-,  7-9,  and  on Encounter  True  Horror,  10+  becomes  10-12,  because  there's  a fourth results range: 13+.

Keep in mind that, similar to Face Peril, you have to pick one of these options - you can't choose not to. You also gain one horror after you roll.

Unlike  Investigate  and  Face  Peril,  this  move  is  entirely addressed at the player - the Oracle doesn't make any decisions on this, no matter the results.

13+

These are the worst possible options. Note that you can't roll one of these the first time you Encounter True Horror! When you roll you add your horror, and since you start with no horror, the most you can roll on your first check is 12. After resolving that  roll,  you  gain  a  horror,  and  next  time  there'll  be  a  one  in thirty six chance you'll roll a 12 and get this result - and it only goes up from there until you Let the Horror Sink In.

- O You lose yourself in the horror. Perhaps you find something compelling in it and switch allegiances, or perhaps you retreat into your mind and never leave the fetal position again, or perhaps you retreat to your room to write a mysterious tome for the rest of your life. Mark this off.

This choice almost certainly removes your character from the game. This is a tragic ending, though many would consider it preferable to dying. This allows you to get really creative with what happens to your character! Come up with something fun and inventive that really digs into the story so far, the setting, and what's happened with your character.

This  result  could  also  represent  your  character  being transformed into a monster - perhaps being replaced with a pod person, or becoming a vampire, or joining the hivemind.

- You're barely holding on. Mark off a positive tag or a town tag. Perhaps you're too traumatized to use a particular skill again,  or  perhaps  later  tonight  you  sleepwalk  and  burn down the town mall, or perhaps you lash out and break your favorite tool in the process.

This is the option you pick if you don't want to retire your character just yet. It's still pretty darn bad, though, representing a permanent loss to your character or the town.

## 10-12

As with all the results ranges on Encounter True Horror, you have to pick one of these options, you can't skip it.

- O You take foolish or selfish action, endangering yourself and / or the people around you. Perhaps you rush at a monster you have no hope of defeating, or perhaps you read the spell you just discovered aloud from the creepy old book you just found before anyone can stop you, or perhaps you attempt to flee in a way that makes it harder for your friends to follow. Mark this off.

This  option  represents  your  character  making  a  bad decision. This is something we see all the time in horror movies characters acting in ways that make for a fun story and also have you throwing popcorn at the screen shouting 'Why would you do that?!' This is your opportunity to live that out.

- O You experience the sudden shock to your system in a way that permanently alters your sense of self. Gain a wild tag, like prophetic  nightmares ,  or foolhardy  bravery ,  or obsessive paranoia . Mark this off.

This option represents a sudden change in your character. Experiencing  something  shocking  can  cram  a  lot  of  character development into a short moment - how do you want to say this affected you?

- Your experiences are wearing on you. Gain a negative tag, like pareidolia ( seeing patterns that aren ' t  there ) ,  or losing moments of  time ,  or  another  trauma  symptom  or  mental  illness  that you as the player want to explore with your character, and which your fellow players and the Oracle are comfortable with having in the game. Recover the other two options.

The recovery option for this results range, this represents the accumulated experience of Encountering True Horror wearing on you and making your life more difficult in some way.

## 7-9

As with all the results ranges on Encounter True Horror, you have to pick one of these options, you can't skip it.

- O You  notice  something  useful  in  the  fear,  and  gain  a  clue. Perhaps you spot a potential weakness in the creature that just surprised you, or notice a useful detail in the instructions for the spell in the tome you just read, or piece together a pattern you hadn ' t noticed before. Ask a question about what you've encountered and mark this off.

This is for when you're observant enough to spot something helpful. A classic Velma moment - while everyone else runs in fear,  you  notice  a  detail  that  will  help  you  unravel  the  whole mystery.  You  get  to  ask  the  Oracle  a  question,  which  they'll answer, though perhaps not completely.

- O You spot an opportunity, and put either yourself or someone else in danger to take advantage of it. Perhaps the spell will require  a  sacrifice,  and  you  don ' t  tell  one  of  your  fellow casters that, or perhaps you can leap in between your allies and the creature to aid their escape, or perhaps you knock one of them down to aid your own. Mark this off.

This  is  your  opportunity  to  either  be  a  selfish  jerk  or  a selfless hero. Do you want to put yourself in danger to help your friends, or will you sacrifice them to save yourself? This choice always makes for great moments at the table, since it involves the other protagonists so deeply.

- Your trauma is starting to pile up. Gain an additional horror. Recover the other two options.

Sometimes  things  just  get  to  you,  man.  At  least  you're getting the other options back.

## 6-

You gain foreboding, and aside from becoming slightly more horrified, nothing else bad happens -yet.

This is the best possible outcome. You gain a horror because you Encountered True Horror (there's no way to avoid that), and you gain a foreboding because you rolled a 6-, which is true for any move you roll on, and nothing else happens. Since foreboding is  helpful,  this  is  as  good  as  it  gets  when  you  Encounter  True Horror.

## Revelations

Revelations are for when you want to have your character change in some specific way. They can be taken during downtime, when you have a while to play out a scene in the present, or they could be flashbacks that reveal more about your character or the world, or they could happen off screen. The point is that they represent  character  or  setting  development  -  revealing  more information, or changing what we already know.

## Let the Horror Sink In

This is the move that lets you get rid of horror, but at a cost. It represents the trauma your character has experienced causing long term effects.

- O You can no longer rely on something you used to depend on. What was it? How will you still make partial use of it, or its replacement, going forward? Mark off a positive tag. Gain a related wild tag. Mark this off.

This option represents  a loss of a physical or mental ability, or an NPC friend, or anything else you choose that you used to rely on that's become unreliable. It is the inevitable degradation of  all  things.  This  could  be  your  car  starting  to  break  down, your mental faculties starting to slip, or your once athletic body starting to be broken down by the whiskey you've been drinking to stave off the demons.

- O Your experiences leave you shaken and with new problems. What are they? Gain a negative tag. Mark this off.

This represents new things affecting you - new problems you have, either within your own mind or body, or with other people. Character development doesn't always have to be good. This could be you gaining a trauama symptom, or an NPC having a problem with you, or anything else that will start to get in your way.

- Your experiences are making you lose part of yourself. Which part, and why? Mark off a positive tag. Recover the other two options.

The  worst  option,  this  represents  a  permanent  loss  of something that was once a defining part of you. This option is a tragedy, and should be treated as such.

## Reveal Something About the World

This move lets you affect things outside of yourself. You can create new people, places, and things with this move - it's a good opportunity to make a big impact on the setting, so have fun and get creative with it.

- There's something new in town - perhaps a new landmark, organization, or prominent player in town. Who or what is it? A biker gang that just rode into town, a new bookstore that opened on Main Street, a famous scientist who just joined the university? The town gains a tag naming them or it.

This  lets  you  create  a  new  town  tag,  like  the  ones  you created during character creation. There are other ways to create new town tags (the Epilogue move, for instance) but this is the one that doesn't require everybody else's consent - you can make up something new entirely on your own. It's nice to still take into account the other players preferences, of course - this is a cooperative game, after all.

- Someone you know comes to town. Who are they? A cousin

from the country, a colleague returning from an expedition, a  touring  musician  you  grew  up  with?  What  is  your relationship like? Gain a wild tag naming them.

This creates a new NPC that you have a special relationship with. If you want the new NPC to just be a new person in town that  you  don't  have  a  strong  connection  to,  you  should  create them as a town tag with the first option, but if you want to have them be specifically connected to your character, you should pick this one.

- Some new and interesting item arrives on the scene. What is it? A rare tome at the University library, a strange statue at the art gallery, a mysterious machine in a warehouse by the docks? If you can manage to gain possession or control of the item, gain a wild tag naming it.

This option creates a new item in the world. It's probably a pretty significant one, that could even spawn the creation of a new mystery later on. Importantly, you don't immediately gain a wild tag representing it the way you do with the NPC created by the previous option, or by an item created with the Take Care of Yourself move. Instead, if you want to use it, you'll need to figure out how to gain access to it within the fiction.

## Reveal Something About Yourself

This is the move that is closest to 'leveling up' that you may be familiar with from other RPGs.

- O You heal from your wounds. How do you do so? Emergency surgery  in  a  hospital,  convalescing  at  home,  black  market opiates? Recover a marked off option on face peril. Mark this off.

This is a one-time use option to heal yourself. This takes you a step back from the brink of death. Note that using this option does not get rid of any tags you gained from the option you recovered - it just lets you pick that option again. You can get rid of those tags with more uses of this move, using one of the below options. Note that this is a one time use option, since you have to mark it off.

You can even use this option to come back from the dead, by recovering the 'You die' option on Face Peril as part of the Epilogue  move!  A  classic  trope  in  horror  movies  is  someone appearing to be killed, but then opening their eyes during the final scene of the film, and this is how you can do that in Fear of the Unknown.

- You're not just trying to recover - you're actively improving. In what way? Practicing with a weapon or martial art, studying a new subject, going shopping? Alternatively, this can also represent revealing an aspect of your past that had not yet been  brought  up  in  the  story.  Gain  a  positive  tag  naming your new (or newly revealed) ability or resource.

This option is the one that's the closest to 'leveling up'. You gain a new skill or resource or connection - or reveal one you already had that just hasn't come up yet in the story. This can be chosen as many times as you have the humanity to pay for it.

- You're working on overcoming your weaknesses. Which one are you currently working on, and how are you improving? Get a cane to help with your chronic limp, go to therapy to rein in your neuroses, pay off a debt?  Mark off a negative tag, and gain a related wild tag.

Another option that  improves  things  for  you,  this  turns what was a liability into a potential asset (though it can still get in your way). You can also pick this option as many times as you have the humanity to pay for it.

## Epilogue

Ideally, each session of Fear of the Unknown should be a complete story, like a single movie (though of course multiple sessions  can  have  multiple  stories  that  build  upon  each  other, like a series of films). At the end of a session / story, you should call for everyone to make this move. It represents a series of short scenes depicting how the characters and town has changed as a result of the events of the story.

The first few options give a bit more experience, so that when they make revelations, they're more likely to have enough of the appropriate forms of experience and make the moves they want.

If you're pressed for time, feel free not to call for this move, or  only  call  for  a  subset  of  the  questions  on  it.  I  prefer  to  ask one of the questions to everyone, get all the answers, then do the  same with the next question, et cetera, but you could also have one player answer all of them at once, then another player answer all of them, et cetera.

The questions here are meant to highlight aspects of the game that  the  players  enjoyed,  cementing  those  in  everyone's minds,  and  to  make  the  Oracle  and  the  other  players  feel appreciated for what they did during the game.

The answers to all of these should be relatively short, and can be the same for different protagonists. You don't want to drag this out too long - this is the last ordinary scene or two of the film, plus some short scenes during and after the credits.

- Is there a particular person, location, organization, or something  else  that  you  encountered  tonight  that  you're certain will be more influential in the town going forward? If all of the players agree on the same answer, gain a town tag representing it.

This question is addressed to all of the players together.

It represents a major change in the status quo of the setting - a new thing of power or importance arising that will be majorly influential in any future games taking place in the same town.

- Describe  the  most  emotionally  significant  moment  in  the game for your protagonist. If it scared your protagonist, gain a horror. If you think there will be effects from it that continue into the future, describe them and gain a foreboding. If you overcame an obstacle, either through your own skill or with help from another protagonist or an NPC, describe how you did it and gain a humanity.

The point of this question is to bring the peak to the players attention at the end of the story (see Peak and End on page 145).

- Make any number of other revelations.

The  point  of  this  option  is  to  give  the  protagonists  the opportunity to create mid and after credits scenes. They should pack a lot of change into a short bit of description.
