## A Game of Intimate Horror

Fear  of  the  Unknown  is  a  game  of  intimate  horror.  It's about how your characters and the place they live are connected, and this terrible thing that happens to them. It's about how living through these events  changes them, as people and as a town.

You'll all collaborate to create the small town setting, and then create characters together (and each character will have at least one bond with another character), and while you're doing this, the Oracle will come up with the prompt and inciting incident for the mystery (see 'The Premise of the Mystery') below. This ensures that everyone is equally familiar with the setting, that the characters are all connected in some way, and that the characters all make sense in the setting.

Because the Oracle comes up with the secret of the mystery during setting and character creation, you end up with a mystery that  is  intimately  connected  to  the  setting  and  characters . Characters should have a personal stake in the mystery, a reason to try to solve it beyond 'that's what we all came here to do in this game'. If nothing else, it's affecting the place that they live - but in most instances, there should be something else, with the mystery either affecting the characters directly or NPCs they care about.

The  mechanics  of  Fear  of  the  Unknown  will  regularly prompt you to make changes to your character - gaining or losing abilities, possessions, or connections, experiencing trauma, and  the  short  and  long  term  consequences  of  that.  These  are opportunities for you to tell your character's story, show their development, and play ou their character arc.

## a Horror Film

Every session of Fear of the Unknown is like a horror film. A campaign then is like a horror series - which could be continuing to battle the same antagonist, like A Nightmare on Elm Street, or a series of thematically related antagonists, like Scream, or a new horrifying mystery every week, like Scooby Doo, Where Are You!, or a series of barely related stories that may or may not share the same characters, like From Beyond the Grave.

Let's take a look at the structure of an individual game of Fear of the Unknown: a mystery with a secret to be uncovered, an inciting incident to draw the players in, and a series of scenes of the protagonists uncovering clues in order to solve the mystery and  force  a  final  confrontation  with  the  antagonist.  Then, the  epilogue,  showing  what  lingering  effects  there  are  on  the characters and setting.

## A Mystery

Fear of the Unknown is a game about horrifying mysteries. If you're like me, the hardest part of writing mysteries is coming up with the clues. Good news: with the Investigate move, you don't have to! Instead, a mystery has just two components, and while you can come up with them ahead of time if you insist, the tools are here to help you come up with them during character and setting creation.

## The Secret

This  is  the  horrible  thing  going  on  that  the  players  will want to uncover. The Oracle will come up with this during town and character creation, and it should be connected to the tags you create during those phases. A secret typically consists of a thing that's happening, who's doing it, and what their motive is.

For instance, a ghost is haunting the mall because it was built on top of the tree the ghost was hanged on, or a deranged child  is  killing  anyone  who  reminds  him  of  his  parents,  or  a conspiracy of the wealthiest people in town is sacrificing people to maintain their fortunes.

For  inspiration  on  creating  the  secret,  see  the  various inspiration tables starting on page 124.

## The Inciting Incident

This is how the players learn that there is a secret to uncover. This will be the first scene after town and character creation, and should almost definitely take place at or involve some of the tags you create during those phases.

This could be as action packed as a masked killer making an attempt on the lives of one or more of the protagonists, or as subtle as overhearing a conversation about people disappearing, or  as  blatant  as  someone  directly  asking  them  to  investigate  a building they claim is haunted.

For  inspiration  on  creating  the  inciting  incident,  see  the table on page 132 .

And that's it  for  the  mystery  premise! After  the  inciting incident, the players should have questions they want to find the answers to. Just ask them what they want to know and how they want to find it out, use the Investigate move, and go from there. When the players have figured out the secret, then it's time to move on to a final confrontation with the antagonist - and the players  will  likely  have  ideas  for  how  they  want  to  go  about doing that.

## 3 act structure

Fear of the Unknown generates a horror mystery story that is similar to a horror film. These stories naturally fall into a three act structure. Your games don't have to follow this structure, but a lot of it will arise naturally just from how the game's mechanics work, and how our minds think of stories, and when in doubt, following it can be an easy way to make a satisfying narrative.

The  first  act  establishes  the  norm  from  which  the  later story will deviate, so that we know what's weird and different, by having something to contrast it with. This is the setting and character  creation  step.  This  establishes  the  setting,  introduces the  protagonists,  shows  us  different  things  about  them,  and introduces the background characters (NPCs), giving us reasons to care about them for when things start going bad later in the story.

## The First Act - Character and Setting Introduction

The first act starts with town and character creation and ends with the inciting incident, which is when we transition from making the setting and characters to playing as those characters in  the  setting.  For  instance,  perhaps  the  protagonists  stumble upon a dead body, or the killer tries to make one of them be the newest victim and they narrowly escape, or they start hearing strange rumours around town.

## The Second Act - Investigation and Change

The  second  act  is  the  rising  action.  This  is  the  initial investigation. The protagonists will try to figure out what's going on and what they need to do about it, which will involve going to different locations, talking with other characters, and potentially entering danger. The protagonists change during this part of the story, and you might have one or more confrontations with the antagonist, as well as the potential for revelations.

This act is made up primarily of the Investigation based core gameplay loop described above and in greater detail on page 140 .

## ThE THird Act - Climax and Epilogue

The  third  act  contains  the  climax  and  conclusion  of  the story, followed by the epilogue. The protagonists are definitely going to have to Face  Peril here.

Because  the  setting  and  character  creation  is  followed by the inciting incident, there will naturally be a split between act  1  and  everything  following  it.  A  good  narrative  device  to help create the feel of more of an arc to the story and to draw a stronger distinction between act 2 and act 3 is to have there be a pseudo-climax: a powerful incident that changes things up and forces the players to come up with a new way of trying to solve their  predicament.  This  is  most  often  a  confrontation  with  the antagonist that doesn't permanently defeat them, and indeed is typically more costly for the protagonists. The important thing is that the players learn something from the encounter, so that they know they have to come up with a different strategy to finally resolve the horror, and that this gives them a starting point to figure out what that change should be. For instance, perhaps what they thought was an ordinary, human serial killer is revealed to be supernatural in some way and so can't be killed by mundane means, or perhaps the person they're convinced is the killer is caught but the murders continue, or perhaps the killer evades their carefully laid trap, implying the existence of a mole in their ranks.

After the  final  confrontation,  where  the  protagonists either succeed or fail at defeating the antagonist, there will be an epilogue. This is when you should go over the changes that have happened to the characters, and call for them to make the epilogue move.
