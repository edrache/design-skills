## The Oracle

If you're going to play as a protagonist, you can go ahead and  skip  this  section  -  this  is  for  the  Oracle,  the  person  who comes  up  with  the  premise  for  the  mystery  and  plays  as  the antagonist, the world, and everything else in the setting besides the protagonists.

As  the  Oracle,  your  job  is  to  set  up  the  premise  of  the mystery (the  secret  and  inciting  incident),  to call  for  moves when the fiction triggers them, then react appropriately to  the outcomes of moves (including making choices on a result of 6- for Investigate and Face Peril), having the fiction change in ways that make sense based on what the players (and NPCs!) do. Finally, arrange for the climax , if the protagonists don't do it themselves.

The next few pages have some tables you can roll on to come up with the premise of your mystery. A mystery has two parts: the secret and the inciting incident.

The secret is  the  horrible  thing  that's  going  on  that  the protagonists  want  to  understand.  It's  who  is  doing  what  and why - the source of the horror, what it is they're doing, and their motive for doing so.

The inciting incident is the first scene in the story, which you play immediately after town and character creation. It's how the protagonists learn that there is a secret to be uncovered. At the end of  the  inciting  incident,  the  protagonists  should  have  questions they want answers to. From there, they use the Investigate move to  create  scenes  to  find  the  answers  to  those  questions,  which lead to more questions, which lead to more investigations, which eventually  lead  to  a  direct  confrontation  with  the  antagonist. Sprinkled throughout this should be moments of downtime or flashbacks  where  they  can  make  revelations  and  have  calmer interactions with the other protagonists or with NPCs.

The confrontation could either end the story, or it could lead to more investigation or preparation, which leads back to another confrontation with the antagonist.

After the final confrontation, the climax to the story, then you should call for the Epilogue move, and play out the changes to the town and characters in a series of short scenes.

## So you need a mystery

Here are some charts to help you come up with a secret and inciting incident for the mystery. A crucial thing to keep in mind when coming up with a mystery is to tie the mystery to the protagonists and town tags .

Coming  up  with  a  mystery  before  you  know  what  the setting  and  characters  will  be  never  works  as  well  as  coming up with it after you have them. The players are a crucial part of coming up with the story, it's not just you doing it, and if the mystery doesn't have anything to do with the setting, why come up with the setting at all?

Fear of the Unknown is a game of intimate horror. What's going  on  should  be  connected  to  the  protagonists.  Even  in  a slasher movie with rather disposable characters, where the killer is just killing for the sake of killing, you can still tie the manner of those deaths to the individuals. A hunter gets caught in his own traps, or the owner of a wax museum gets dipped in wax alive, or an athlete gets chased by an inexhaustible killer for miles before collapsing from exhaustion.

These charts are not meant to be exhaustive. They're just a tool that, if you use it, you'll consistently be able to come up with a good mystery for your game. If you can come up with a good mystery without using it, more power to you! And of course you don't necessarily have to use whatever you roll - feel free to reroll or just pick another option if it speaks to you more in the moment.

You  can  also  roll  multiple  times  and  combine  them  for more complex mysteries!

Just  before  or  at  the  beginning  of  town  and  character  creation, roll once on each of the following tables - the Antagonist, What They're Doing , their Motive , and the Inciting Incident .

Keep what you've rolled in mind during town and character creation. Come up with ways to make it be tied together with what the players are coming up with. Every part should be an

aspect of a cohesive whole.

For instance, if the antagonist is haunting somewhere, it should probably be the landmark, or a location that's important to one or more of the protagonists.

If the antagonist is trying to kill people (as they often are), make at least one of those people be someone at least one of the

protagonists cares about.

## The Antagonist

Roll a single 6 sided die to get a random antagonist.

1.  A Mundane Killer
2. A Seemingly Unstoppable Killer
3.  An Unbelievable Supernatural Killer
4.  A Cosmic Horror
5.  An Enormous Threat
6. A Conspiracy

A  mundane  killer  is  a  regular  person  who's  trying  to commit murder (or do something similar - see 'what are they doing'  below).  Examples  include  Pamela  Voorhees  from  the original Friday the 13th, the various Ghostfaces from the Scream films, and Norman Bates from Psycho.

A  seemingly  unstoppable  killer  is  someone  who  may appear ordinary at first, but is much harder to kill than a normal person, and may have other powers - most often super strength. Examples  include  Jason  Voorhees,  especially  from  the  later Friday the 13th films, Michael Meyers from the Halloween films, and many of the classic Universal Monsters, such as the Wolf Man or Dracula.

An unbelievable supernatural killer is a magical monster that  doesn't  necessarily  even  appear  human.  They  can  do impossible things and go nearly anywhere they like, but should be constrained by specific rules that the protagonists can discover. Examples  include  Pennywise  the  Dancing  Clown  from  It,  the Leprechaun  from  the  Leprechaun  series,  and  Freddy  Krueger from the Nightmare on Elm Street series.

A cosmic horror is something beyond human comprehension.  A  great  deal  of  the  fear  in  a  cosmic  horror story comes from the realization that forces in scale far beyond the  scope  of  what  the  protagonists  start  out  even  thinking  are possible are at work. Examples include Cthulhu from The Call of Cthulhu, Sutter Cane from In the Mouth of Madness, and the Necronomicon Ex Mortis from The Evil Dead.

An  enormous  threat is a supernatural  or  mundane antagonist that's either a singular enormous creature or a huge number of creatures acting in concert, or something in between. Examples include the birds in The Birds, the graboids from the Tremors series, and the blob from The Blob.

A conspiracy is a group of people working together. They could be a cult with access to magic powers, or just ordinary people using teamwork towards malicious ends. Examples include the locals in 2000 Maniacs, the eponymous Children of the Corn, and the Sawyer family in The Texas Chain Saw Massacre.

For more details and examples, see the Videography on page 226.

## What are they doing?

Roll a single 6 sided die to get inspiration for what they're doing.

1.  Seemingly Random Killings
2. Highly Ritualized Killings
3.  Obvious Killings
4.  Kidnappings
5.  Coercing People
6.  Haunting a Specific Location

Seemingly  random  killings  at  first  present  no  pattern. You'll have to investigate to have any idea why the victims were chosen or who the next victim might be. The connection could be as simple as 'they had their lights on on Halloween' like in Halloween, or there could be a secret that connects them, like 'their parents killed the killer years ago, and now his ghost is killing their children' like in A Nightmare on Elm Street.

Highly ritualized killings are ones where it's obvious how, or at least that, they're connected. They could all be killed in the same elaborate way, like in Halloween 3: Season of the Witch, or they could all be killed in thematically related ways, like in Se7en.

Obvious killings are when someone just goes on a spree. Examples include the Friday the 13th series and pretty much any other movie set at a campground.

Kidnappings are when the antagonist takes people away from wherever they're attacked. They could be replacing them, like in Invasion of the Body Snatchers, or perhaps holding them somewhere to torture them, like in Saw, or perhaps feeding off them, like in Dracula or Cannibal Holocaust, or anything else, as long as they don't leave a corpse behind for people to discover after the attack.

<!-- image -->

Coercing people is when the antagonist gets people to do things they normally wouldn't, and that they might not want to do. They could be blackmailing them like in Blackmail, or perhaps brainwashing them like in Shiver, or perhaps transforming them into monsters like in Zombi 2 (or any other zombie movie), or

anything else that makes good people untrustworthy.

Haunting a specific location is when the antagonist tries to get people to leave a place alone like in The Legend of Hell House, or repair some damage or insult done to it, like in Poltergeist.

## Their Motive

Roll a single 6 sided die to get inspiration for the reason the antagonist is doing what they're doing.

1.  Revenge
2. Obsession
3.  Power
4.  Jealousy
5.  Fear
6. If they do have a motive, it's beyond the comprehension of regular people

Alternatively, roll on this table:

1.  Love
2. They can't help it
3.  Delusion
4.  They were framed (why? roll again!)
5.  There's more than one of them
6. It's a trick

Revenge is simple: they or someone they care about were wronged  in  the  past,  and  now  they're  taking  vengeance,  but not necessarily on anyone it makes sense to blame. Perhaps the victims are descended from the people who hurt them, like in A Nightmare on Elm Street, or perhaps they just have some traits in common, like Friday the 13th.

Obsession is when they have a fixation on something, such as a specific victim, like in Hitcher, or the acts themselves, like in Terrifier.

Power is when the antagonist acts to gain more power for themselves, such as in Rosemary's Baby or The Invisible Man.

Jealousy is when the antagonists acts out of a desire for something they do not, and perhaps could never possess, such as any murder for profit, like The Changeling or the Amazing Bulk.

An incomprehensible or nonexistant motive could be that they don't have any motive, like in the original Halloween, or that they are utterly alien, like in Color Out of Space.

Love  is  the  motive  for  plots  enacted  to  benefit  another. This could be a desire to protect, like in Scooby Doo on Zombie Island, or out of a misplaced idea of what benefits them, like in The Mummy.

They can't help it if they'd rather not commit these crimes, but can't help themselves. Perhaps it's instinct, like in The Wolf Man,  or  perhaps  they're  under  another's  control,  like  in  The Incredibly Strange Creatures Who Stopped Living and Became Mixed-Up Zombies?!.

If they were framed, then there should be early, obvious clues that point to a specific person as the antagonist, only for them  to  be  revealed  to  be  innocent!  Roll  again  to  see  what's really happening. Perhaps it was a series of innocent mistakes, like in the Slumber Party Massacre remake, or perhaps it was a deliberate framing, like in The Bird With the Crystal Plumage.

If  there's more than one antagonist, you'll have to figure out  if  they're  working  together,  like  in  Scream,  or  if  they're unaware of what each other is doing, like in Curtains. You'll also have to decide if it's just a few people working together, or if it's the whole town, like in 2000 Maniacs (though this really starts to bleed into 'conspiracy' as the type of antagonist - though that's okay! Like I said, none of this is set in stone the way the player moves are).

If  it's  a  trick,  whatever  it  is  that  seems  to  be  happening really isn't. Maybe it's a prank, like in the beginning of Friday the 13th part 3, or maybe it's a delusion, like in the early parts of The Shining. What is really going on?

## Inciting Incident

Roll a single 6 sided die to get inspiration for the inciting incident - the first scene after town and character creation, and how the protagonists learn that there is a secret to be uncovered.

1.  The protagonists stumble upon a victim
2. Someone invites them to investigate
3. The protagonists are (attempted) victims
4.  A friend or enemy is a victim
5.  Rumours start going around
6. Something happens at or to one of the town tags

The protagonists stumble upon a victim - perhaps someone escaping  the  killer,  like  in  the  remake  of  The  Texas  Chainsaw Massacre, or perhaps they witness a murder from afar, like in Deep  Red.  This  is  a  classic  way  to  start  things  off,  providing enough to prompt questions without directly endangering the protagonists just yet.

Someone invites  them  to  investigate  if  they  are  literally asked by an NPC to figure out what's going on, like in Ghostbusters or The Legend of Hell House. This is a good fit for a group of protagonists who are professional investigators or mediums or the monster hunters / debunkers.

The protagonists are attempted victims when the inciting incident is the antagonist directly attacking them. Any movie that starts in media res is like this - pick a Friday the 13th. This is a fun way to jump into the action right away.

A  friend  or  enemy  is  the  victim  when  someone  the protagonists know is one of the victims, like in Ring or Scream. This provides a natural, strong connection between the protagonists and the story, and it's a good choice to start off nearly any story.

The  inciting  incident  being  rumours  means  that  the players hear that something might be going on before anything definitively does. This is a more subtle way to get things started, and can be tougher to run - it requires inquisitive protagonists who seek out answers or action without necessarily being handed a strong motivation. Examples include 13 Ghosts and Candyman.

Having something happen at or to one of the town tags is a great way to start off the game in a manner that immediately ties in the setting you've collaboratively created. This could be anything from someone vandalizing it, to seeing a strange light or other evidence of a haunting there, to a murder taking place there  as  the  opening  scene.  It  probably  shouldn't  yet  involve the destruction of the town tag, but it could foreshadow its later destruction - or even in the opening scene, if they have to Face Peril and choose to mark off a town tag. This combines well with any of the other rolls - when in doubt as to how to start, it's hard to go wrong with with this plus another roll.

## Why won't the police help?

The  protagonists,  after  encountering  these  horrors  and realizing the danger that they're in, may want to go to the police for help. But just like in horror films and real life, that's unlikely to be helpful. If they have a tag that indicates a bond with the police, or they are cops, or the police are represented by a town tag, then they can invoke them that way. Otherwise, if they do want to go to the police, roll a single 6 sided die for inspiration as to why the police won't be helpful.

1.  They're in on it
2. They're being bribed or threatened
3.  They try to help, and are immediately killed
4.  They don't believe the protagonists
5.  They're out of contact
6. They're incompetent

If  they're  in  on  it,  that  means  they're  working  with  the antagonist  in  some  way.  Perhaps  they  are  the  antagonists,  or perhaps they're the antagonist's underlings, or perhaps they just sympathize with them.

If  they're  being  bribed,  then  while  they  don't  have  a personal stake in the antagonist's actions, they're being paid not to do anything about it, and they're not about to jeopardize that paycheck. Alternatively, perhaps they're being threatened, and won't act out of fear, rather than greed.

If they try to help and are immediately killed, well, that's fairly self explanatory. This is my favorite option, because it gives the protagonists a glimmer of false hope before yanking it away and revealing that the protagonists are on their own, and that the antagonist is more dangerous than they'd thought.

If they don't believe the protagonists, that's another classic scenario:  the  protagonists  go  to  the  police,  and  the  police  just dismiss them. This could go as far as arresting the protagonists for making false reports (leaving them vulnerable and helpless in a jail cell to whatever the antagonist does next), or just laugh them out of the police station. This is a great choice to stir up anger in the protagonists and impress upon them that they're the only ones who can put a stop to this. This option nearly always comes  up  with  an  unbelievable  supernatural  killer  (that's  the 'unbelievable' part) but they could just as well adamantly refuse

to believe there's a mundane serial killer in their small town.

If  they're  out  of  contact,  that  could mean that they're all mysteriously missing, or they're all out working on other cases, or perhaps the protagonists just don't have cell reception. In any

case, they're unreachable, and won't be coming to help.

If they're incompetent, then they may try to help, but just can't actually do anything useful. This can be a good way to inject

some levity and humor into an otherwise dour story.

## Running The Game

So you and the other players have made a town together, they each have a protagonist, and you've come up with a secret and inciting incident. You're ready to run the inciting incident, where they may have to Face Peril, but that definitely won't be the end of it, so, now what?

## Core Gameplay Loop

The idea of the 'core gameplay loop' is to have a structure that lets you ensure there's never a dull moment: there's always something for you to fall back on doing, to ensure that there's always  something  going  on.  There  can  be quiet moments  moments where there's nothing exciting happening, things are restful - but there should never be dull moments - times when the players are at a loss for what to do.

After the inciting incident, the players  should  have questions, and they enter the core gameplay loop, which goes like this:

Whenever the players have questions they want answers to, they should use the Investigate move to set up a new scene. Then, they should either have new questions, or have something they want to do - most likely confronting the antagonist.

If the players are ever unsure what to do, ask them what they want to know ,  then use their answer to construct a scene with  the  Investigate  move.  That's  the  loop!  Questions  lead  to investigations  lead  to  questions  lead  to  investigations  leads, eventually, to a confrontation with the antagonist.

If they ever don't have any questions, then they should be ready for a confrontation with the antagonist (which could be the final confrontation, or one confrontation of many). If they're not sure if they're ready, too bad! Spring the confrontation on them anyway. Hell, do that whenever they start dragging their feet, if they're not sure of what questions to ask. The antagonist can always escape and lead them on another trail of clues again, getting you right back into the Investigation loop.

The Investigation loop is what makes up the bulk of act 2. For more on the 3 act structure, see page 25.

At all times you need to react to what the players do and call for the appropriate moves whenever they're triggered.

## Clues

One of the most important parts of running the game is coming up with clues. This works a bit differently than you might be used to. The first thing to keep in mind is that you should not come up with clues ahead of time . Instead, your clue should be based on the question the player is asking and the tags they invoked for the check .

A  clue  is  an  answer,  partial  or  complete,  to  a  player's question .  When a player gains a clue, they ask you a question, and you should either answer it, or give them some information and tell them how they can learn more. A clue is not just 'you see this specific piece of physical remains', it should be an answer to their question.

Your goal, as the Oracle, is to give the players information . One of the most common problems for new Oracles is that they will give very vague clues, often in the form of physical evidence the antagonist left behind, like 'a size 12 bootprint', which may be enough for an actual trained detective to go off of, but is not enough for regular people to figure things out from.

The questions the players ask need to be in the nice middle ground of not too specific and not too vague. This isn't normally a problem, but just in case, here are some guidelines to keep in mind. Questions should be narrow enough that no single clue would be enough to solve the mystery on its own, and broad enough  that  every  clue  should  bring  the  players  closer  to solving the mystery . No clue should be worthless. If the players don't know what a clue is telling them, talk it through with them until they do. A narrow question, when broadly answered, can be a good clue. Figuring out how to do this well is one of your core skills as an Oracle.

Whenever a player asks a question, your answer should always either lead to them drawing a conclusion and wanting to confront the antagonist, or it should lead to them asking new questions.

For  instance,  consider  if  the  players  are  investigating  a crime  scene.  What  happened  here  is  that  an  interdimensional alien  got  inside  someone's body and expanded, blowing them up from the inside. The players don't know this yet. They see that there's blood and gore all over the place, emanating from a central location, indicating an explosion.

They ask 'can I figure out what caused this explosion?' You don't want to just say 'yes, it was an alien expanding inside the guy's body.' You also don't want to just say 'no'. Instead, you need to tie the tags they used into the clue they find and tell them something useful based on the tags they picked.

For instance, if  they  picked observant and I  know  folks  on the police force ,  then perhaps they get to listen to what the police are saying about the crime scene and they get to spot details the police missed. You could reply 'The cops are talking to each other at the crime scene, and since you know them they let you take a look too. One of them is holding back wretching, and another is calmly sipping coffee. 'Jesus Christ, it's like a bomb went off inside the guy!' one of them says. And yeah, the explosion pattern looks symmetrical - it doesn't look like there was a bomb in front of someone that went off. But looking more closely, you don't see any scorch marks - it doesn't look like a bomb went off at all, it looks like something just popped the guy from the inside out.'

This gives them new questions - what kind of thing could pop someone from the inside - but doesn't directly solve the mystery - 'yeah it was an alien growing inside them'.

Consider giving them something to look for in the future . Perhaps the alien has a distinctive smell that you can describe, and then reuse that description in later scenes when they're near the alien and might not realize it.

## Confrontation

At some point, the protagonists will either find out enough to confront the antagonist, or they'll be attacked while they're still trying to figure out what's going on, or their paths will cross in some other way.

When that happens, they'll almost certainly need to Face Peril, and quite likely have to Encounter True Horror.

The  important  thing  about  any  confrontation  with  the antagonist  is  that  it  should  change  things  up. After  a  confrontation, the protagonists should have new questions or new goals , and you should go back into the core gameplay loop.

The climax is the final confrontation with the antagonist. Either the antagonist is defeated, or the protagonists die, or they flee  in  terror  and  manage to escape (or do they..?), or in some other way the story comes to a definitive close.

After the climax is resolved, you'll have the opportunity to call for an Epilogue, giving the players a chance to contribute to explaining how the protagonists and the setting have changed as a result of the events of the story. You should also feel free to  contribute  to  this  outside  of  what  the  players  choose  in  the Epilogue move - it's your story too!

## Setup and Payoff

One  of  the  main  things  that  makes  stories  satisfying  is when they have good setup and payoff: when the things that we establish early in the story come into play later in the story in exciting and interesting ways. Setup and payoff is important to your game, and you should make liberal use of it whenever you can.

Setup and payoff is baked into the rules of Fear of the Unknown :  the  narrative  elements  (tags)  that  get  used  during gameplay (payoff) are established either during character creation or later as the result of moves (setup). Because you're going to be building a lot of scenes out of tags and the Investigate move, this means that you'll be necessarily paying off a lot of payoff from earlier.

The  other  thing  that's  worth  thinking  about  in  terms  of setup and payoff is the ultimate confrontation with the antagonist. That's the ultimate payoff to the story, so for maximum impact, you really want to have the things that come into play be set up ahead of time. The tags will help you with that, and it's also worth putting more thought into it.

Whatever items or NPCs come into play during the final confrontation  should  be  ones  that  were  important  before,  or perhaps ones whose absence was conspicuous - mentioned early on,  and  then  the  players  wondered  why  they  didn't  come  up earlier. The players will naturally help you with this, by showing you  what  elements  of  the  story  they  consider  important  and emotionally significant.

The big pitfall to avoid is the deus ex machina: an arbitrary solution that comes out of nowhere and doesn't have anything to  do  with  what's  happened  already  in  the  story.  This  can  be good for a laugh, but is generally narratively unsatisfying and frustrating,  and  can  make  the  players  feel  like  what  they  did didn't matter or affect things, and that's the opposite of how you want them to feel.

## Peak and End

There's  a  well  documented  psychological  phenomenon people have when they experience something where they don't really  consider  the  whole  experience  when  judging  how  they feel about it. Instead, what sticks most is two parts: the peak (the most emotionally significant thing that happened) and the end (the last thing that happened).

You should make use of this phenomenon whenever you can when running games. It will make it easier for you to create games that your players will remember fondly and excitedly and look back on positively, or which create a feeling of dread that really sticks with them and makes them think.

The key to using this effectively is to make sure that there is at least one really exciting, interesting scene before the ending, and then the ending should go out on a high note. It's okay if you stumble in between those things or when getting to the first really exciting scene: as long as at least one scene is really impactful and the climax is strong, people will like it.

The climax to a film is often not the final scene - there's frequently a denouement afterwards, represented in Fear of the Unknown by the Epilogue move. The Epilogue move is meant to draw to mind the Peak again , and remind the players of what they enjoyed most about the game, as well as have one last bit of creative storytelling by the players. It is important, however, to make sure the Epilogue doesn't take too long, and especially doesn't become boring. I find the most effective Epilogues take around 15 minutes, or perhaps a bit more with more players.

## A and B

A classic storytelling structure that naturally incorporates setup and payoff is the use of A and B plots. This is when you have  two  simultaneous  stories  playing  out,  cutting  between them, which then connect in the climax in a way that resolves both of them. When done right, this is very satisfying, and makes everything that happened in both storylines feel more significant and make everything feel like a natural, cohesive world.

A classic trope in horror stories is the characters splitting up to look for clues.

In  Fear  of  the  Unknown, you have a maximum of three positive tags that can be used to Investigate, no matter how many people help. So if you have more than three people cooperating (or  more than four if you want to let the lead investigator not contribute  a  tag),  you  are  pretty  strongly  incentized  to  split up  and  make  multiple  simultaneous  Investigations,  and  thus different, simultaneous scenes.

It can be tempting, and in many cases is the right decision, for the protagonists to reunite as soon as possible after having different  scenes,  to  compare  notes  and  cooperate  in  different ways going forward.

However, it is sometimes good to have the players split into two groups for a significant portion of the story, especially in larger groups, and develop two simultaneous storylines - until the final confrontation, where they reunite and use their mutual knowledge and preparations to triumph. Or they all get killed whether they're together or alone, whatever works.

## Love and Hate

These  are  the  two  emotions  you  want  to  evoke  in  your players when it comes to NPCs (not counting fear). They should either love them or hate them. The absolute worst thing is for the players to not have any opinion about your non-player characters. If they don't care about what happens to the characters, then you can't use them to any affect in your story.

Antagonists that you love are awesome villains that you want  to  see  do  more  things,  or  even  return  in  future  stories. Antagonists that you hate are horrible monsters that you can't wait to see get their comeuppance, and will really feel motivated to give them what they deserve. In either case, you care about what happens to them.

Bystanders  that you  love  are fan-favorite characters, innocent pure cinnamon buns whom you must protect. If they get  hurt,  that's  serious  business.  Bystanders  that  you  hate  are those assholes that you can't wait to see get killed. Combining these  two  can  be  incredibly  effective  -  having  a  character  the players dislike be mean to a character the players like. There's very little  more  satisfying  than  watching  someone  who's  been a jerk to someone you care about get cut in half by a machete wielding maniac.

In contrast, if the players don't have strong feelings about an antagonist, then they won't feel as motivated to stop them, it  won't  be  as  dramatic  when  they  appear,  and  it  won't  be  as satisfying if and when they are defeated.

If  they  don't  have  strong  feelings  about  the  bystanders, then they won't have as powerful a reaction if they're endangered, won't feel  as  motivated  to  protect  them,  and  won't  remember them as distinctly once the game is over.

One reliable technique for getting players to care about an antagonist is to have them be a character they initially like and think fondly of, who betrays them. This shouldn't be a character that they have as a positive tag, unless the player chooses to mark off that tag as the result of a move, but they could be one of the town tags, or another character who you establish early on.

A shortcut to getting people to care about other characters is to have them be children or animals, like a pet dog. You should be careful with this (see lines and veils in the safety tools 'Safety Tools' on page 19). Everyone gets angry when a dog dies.

Another,  less dangerous  way  to  get  people  to care about  characters  is  to  show  them  caring  about  someone  else. Establishing that an old couple loves each other before they get offed, showing someone sacrifice himself to save his boyfriend, anything that shows what they care about will help.

## Hack this Game

One of the best things about games is customizing them. Everyone who plays Fear of the Unknown will customize it to some degree - just by making their own characters and mysteries, if nothing else. But some folks will want to customize it further, or even create new games out of it. I fully support this, and this section is here to help make that easier for you. If you do make your own custom moves, settings, or entire hacks, please tell me about them! My email is thomas@sixpencegames.com, and I also invite  you  to  share  them  on  the  official  Fear  of  the  Unknown discord (see page 242).

The way you're most likely going to want to hack the game is by making custom moves or custom settings, or changing the character creation questions.

Settings should  provide  tags  that  define  not  the  entire setting, but the parts the protagonists are most likely to interact with.  They  should  be  questions,  so  the  players  can  create  the specifics.

Moves are for bits of narrative structure that you want to have be important and have the mechanics affect - ways that you want to have the game help create stories that have that kind of scenes.

Character  creation  questions are  for  when  you  want  to tell stories focused on a specific kind of character trait, or about groups of people who all have something in common.

So with that in mind, let's make a new character creation question, make some new settings, and make a few custom moves. Feel free to include any of these in your games if they make it more to your (and your players!) liking, or make something new and entirely your own.
