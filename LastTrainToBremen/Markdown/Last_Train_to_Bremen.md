## HERE ' S THE SITUATION

Last Train to Bremen is a storytelling game about doomed musicians fleeing from a cursed contract. To tell this sorry tale, you will need:

- ✶ Four willing participants
- ✶ Thirteen six-sided dice, and a way for players to conceal them from each other
- ✶ One copy of each musician's playsheet (four total)
- ✶ Roughly three hours of your time


## A FEW QUICK POINTERS

Think of this game text like the first thirty pages of a one-act play.

The rules provide a starting place, but it is your responsibility to flesh out the characters, to finish the script, and to play the story to its grim conclusion. Over the course of the game, players will don the roles of playwright, director, performer, and audience alike. Your agenda is to think on your feet, to support and challenge your fellow players, and to tell an exciting story together.


## This is a game of unresolved tension, hidden secrets, and bombastic revelations.

If you want to go in fully unspoiled, don't read too far ahead in the book, and don't read the character sheets until you start playing.

## You can play this game by reading the rules in order.

One section at a time, without a lot of jumping back and forth. Pass the rulebook around the table and take turns reading aloud as you go.


## A NOTE ON CONTENT

Last Train to Bremen is a tragedy about a dysfunctional band of lowlifes. These characters are fucking each other and fucking each other over, often at the same time. The fiction and rules of this game gesture at the following subjects:

- ✶ Smoking and drinking
- ✶ Band hookups and bad breakups
- ✶ Sex, drugs, rock and roll
- ✶ Hedonism and debauchery
- ✶ Vulgar language
- ✶ Deceit and betrayal

It is our collective responsibility to explore these topics in a way that everyone playing will enjoy.


It's helpful to address other players by character name when you're playing out a scene together, but use their personal names if you want to check in with them out-of-character. You can do this for your own role in the band as well: 'This is what I think happened, personally, but my character believes something different…'

If your group has other safety or support tools you like to use, now is a good moment to bring those to the table. Some options include setting a rating like you would for a movie or a TV show (like 'rated M for Mature' or 'rated PG-13, but swearing is okay'); naming lines and veils (like 'implied drug usage is fine, but don't describe anything in detail'); or using an X Card or calling out 'pause' to pump the brakes if a scene gets too intense. Use whichever tools will be most effective and supportive for your table.

## THE CONTRACT

The inciting incident? That's easy. It all comes back to that night in the basement.

The band was together already, by then, playing shit gigs here and there, but that dingy basement cemented us. It bound us together for real, by blood and by ritual. None of that parlor trick shit: real candles, real book, real circle of chalk, and the real fucking Devil showing up with a bargain too good to refuse.

With the gift of hindsight, it's easy to say that signing the contract was a stupid choice. But as some of us might tell you: When all the roads ahead are bad, at least pick one that makes for a compelling story.


## THE BAR

It's hard to believe the Black Barn lasted as long as it did. Fifteen, maybe twenty years of Hound's brother working the bar with the same torn posters littering the walls, same cheap beer on tap, same greasy couch with bad springs in the green room backstage.

To others, the Black Barn is little more than a dive bar, but it's the place that the four of us first met for drinks. The venue for our first paying gig. The place we circled back to, again and again. For all that stardom changed us, the Black Barn always stayed the same. A home, of sorts.

Makes no difference now. It's all gone up in smoke.


## THE TRAIN

The boxcar that the four of us sit in now is a callous, uncharitable thing. It is packed with pallets of freight: paper goods, metal ingots, sacks of grain, lumber. When Mule bribed the man at the trainyard to let us sneak aboard, we did not have the luxury of being choosy.

We have pushed one pallet against the wall to carve out what little elbow room we can. Cat had the foresight to snag a lantern and a book of matches. Our instrument cases are stacked on top of each other as a makeshift table: There is almost enough room for all of us to sit uncomfortably.

The train's swaying and rolling have settled in now: They are a low, constant rumble. Cockerel paces, as much as one can pace in here. Occasionally, through the slats in the side of the car, Hound can see distant lights in the darkness.

There is little to do here besides sit, and wait, and bicker about all the choices we took that led us down this sorry path.


It's an old game; we play it like routine.

## LIAR ' S DICE

Liar's Dice is a class of bluffing games where players roll pools of dice and place wagers about the number of dice that share a particular face value. It is an old game with many names and many variations - but for the purposes of playing Last Train to Bremen , the rules are as follows.

Each player starts with a hand of three dice. Everyone rolls, concealing their dice from the other players. The player who leads the round wagers that of all the dice on the table, a given quantity share a particular face value, e.g., 'three twos.' Going around the table, the next player must raise by making a higher call:

- Either the same quantity of dice, with ✶ a higher face value,
- Or a higher quantity of dice, with any ✶ single face value.


You can increase by as much as you want. If a player says 'three fours,' you can raise to three fives, or three sixes, or four of any value, or five of a kind, or even more. But you can't go backwards: If someone calls 'three ones,' you can't back down to two dice. If a player calls 'four sixes,' the next call must be at least five dice.

## CHALLENGES

At any point, any player may challenge the current call. When somebody issues a challenge, all players reveal their hands and put forward any dice that match the current wager.

The challenger counts up the dice in play:

- If the defender meets or exceeds ✶ the call that they made, they win the round; the challenger loses a die.
- If the defender comes up short of their ✶ wager, the challenger wins the round; the defender loses a die.

When you lose a die, discard it from your hand, decreasing the total number of dice in play.


## WILD VALUES

The final die in the center of the table is the Devil's Die. At the start of a round, the leading player rolls the Devil's Die, out in the open; the resulting value is wild, and any dice that share this value - including the Devil's Die itself - are included in the final count.

However, if a player calls the value on the Devil's Die while wagering, that value stops being wild for the rest of the round: In this case, do not put forward any wild dice during the final count.

After a round ends, the winner of the round makes the opening call during the next round. Continue until all players, save one, have been eliminated.

Still wrapping your head around it? Try playing a couple of practice rounds together. First, play a round or two without the Devil's Die-don't think about wild values. Once you've got your bearings, reset your dice pools and introduce the thirteenth die to get a sense of how wild values change the strategy.


## EXAMPLES IN ACTION




Fox and Crow decide to pass some time with a quick round of dice. With three dice apiece and the Devil between them, they start with a pool of seven dice total. Each rolls their hand, and the Devil's Die starts with a wild value of three.

Fox leads with a modest bet of two fours. The next raise must be two fives or two sixes (the same quantity, with a higher face value), or three or more dice of any number (a higher quantity of any value).

Crow decides to raise to two sixes. Six is the highest face value, so the next raise must be at least three dice, though they may be of any value.

Fox raises to three twos. The next raise could be three threes, three fours, etc.

Threes are wild, so the players count up all the threes and sixes combined; with a total of five, Crow handily meets their goal. Fox gives up a die. Both players reroll their remainder, and Crow leads the next round.

From three twos, Crow raises to three sixes.

Fox raises to four twos.

Crow raises to four sixes.

Fox pushes again, raising to five twos. Crow challenges.

## ROLES

This is the moment to figure out who's playing who. If anyone has strong preferences, make those known now. Divvy up the four roles and hand out their playsheets; everybody gets one. Keep your sheet to yourself.

Hand three dice to each player, and place the Devil's Die in the center of the table where everyone can see.

In character order, take turns reading the narration text ('Here is the truth I conceal within my heart…') on your playsheet aloud.

Take turns asking the questions on your sheet to your fellow players. Ask these questions in rounds, in character order, following the structure listed; every player should ask the first question on their sheet before anybody asks their second. Ask a follow-up question or two if you want, but keep it brief.


When you ask and answer questions, don't worry about trying to be clever or interesting. An answer that seems obvious to you might take your fellow players by complete surprise. Come up with answers that are clear and easy to remember, so you can pull on them later as the story progresses.

Feel free to describe what your musician looks like or what they're carrying, but if you find yourself asking questions such as 'what kind of music do we play?' or 'what decade is it?' you'll want a little more buy-in from the whole table. Come up with something together that everyone can riff on.


## SECRETS

Every member of the band harbors a secret: a hidden truth about themself or their relationship to the world. Once you've answered all of your questions, look at the secrets on your playsheet. Pick one to be a true statement, and feel free to disregard the others.

Each secret comes with a corresponding special mechanic that can be used later in the game. You'll reveal your secret and your special ability after you lose your first two dice. You can hint at them as you play, but don't reveal them until instructed.


✷

Once you've all picked your roles, answered your questions, and chosen your secrets, you should have a pretty good sense of who these characters are and how they relate to one another. Now is a great moment to take a breather and grab some refreshments before you dive into what comes next.


## ACT II. THE BOXCAR

The player who most recently rode a train leads the first hand of Liar's Dice. Play until somebody loses a die. Each time a player loses a die, narrate a memory between the challenger and the defender.

## MEMORIES

Memories are flashbacks: moments between band members, back when things were good - or at least better than they are now. The player giving up a die takes control of the scene. Find the prompts on your sheet that correspond to the die you're losing. Use either or both prompts to narrate a scene between the challenging and defending characters. Describe what you said, what you did, and how this moment changed the relationship between you forever.


## When you narrate a memory, you're in full control.

Other players might chime in with flavor or snide comments - you can even ask them for suggestions, if you want - but you have final say. This is your memory, the way you remember it.

## Keep memories short: two to three minutes tops.

Don't linger in sentimentality. Cut straight to the juiciest piece of the action, and cut away as soon as you feel you've learned something new.

After you've said your piece, your partner may chime in with a brief rebuttal, two or three sentences, to shed light on that memory from their point of view. Once they've finished, they lead the next round.

Every time you win a die off another player, take it and set it off to the side. You'll need those spoils later.


When Fox gives up their first die, the prompt is 'a hasty promise; unearned confidence.' Fox describes a disappointing gig and a moment where they assured Crow that their next crowd would have a good turnout.

Crow responds with a quick jab: 'You might claim you were reassuring me, but be honest: You were only ever trying to convince yourself.' The memory ends. Crow leads the next round.

## TIPS FOR MEMORIES

- Always establish where your memory ✶ takes place.
- Add sensory details. Describe what ✶ your character smells, tastes, feels, or hears.
- Memories are intimate, nonlinear, and ✶ deeply subjective. Use montage and jump back and forth in time. Embrace being an unreliable narrator.
- You may appear in other players' ✶ memories, but you're only guaranteed your own. Make them count.


## REVEALING SECRETS

Eventually, somebody will wind down to their final die. When this happens to you, play out your memory and rebuttal as usual. Then, confess your secret and reveal your ability to the rest of the table.

After you've revealed your ability, you may choose to use it at any time, as long as you're still in the running. Some special abilities can only be used once per game, while others have an ongoing effect. Each ability is marked to indicate its use.

Once you've revealed your secret and ability, continue to the next round.


## RUNNING OUT OF DICE

After you give up your final die and narrate your final memory, return to the present moment. Describe what it looks like when the Devil claims you.

Losing all your dice doesn't mean you're gone forever. You may still linger in other players' memories. You can heckle and jeer, slinging petty barbs and taunts at those who still have skin in the game.

But you're out of the running. You have no say in what happens next.


## ACT III. THE FINAL CONFRONTATION

You know the way these stories go: They whittle and wheedle and wear each other down until eventually, only one musician remains. One last vestige of the band's original greatness. Scarred and battered, haunted by their sins, but alive - and intact. That glimmer of hope grows ever brighter as the train approaches its destination.

Oh, if only it were ever actually that easy.


In the final confrontation, the Devil arrives in spectacular fashion to confront the last player standing - the Survivor -head-on.

The other players are now thrust into the role of the Devil's three heads: lingering grudges, manifestations of spite, resentful specters of those foolish bandmates who have already been claimed, warped, and twisted to aid in the Devil's revenge.

Remember earlier, when we told you to keep track of the dice you won in previous rounds? Here's where they come in. Survivor, take all the dice you won when you knocked them from other players' hands. Divide your spoils between the other players as evenly as you can. (If it's an uneven number, skew toward the players who you actually won those dice from, but make sure everybody gets at least one.) Everyone else can clear out their spoils; you won't need those anymore.

Remove the Devil's Die from the table, and cross out your special ability. You cannot rely on such petty trickery to save you now. You must face the Devil alone.


You're still playing Liar's Dice, but the rules of the game have changed:

- As before, each player rolls the dice ✶ in front of them. It's three against one now: Each Devil's head rolls separately, but all three are teaming up against the Survivor.
- Rather than going around the table ✶ in a circle, alternate back and forth between the Survivor and each Devil's head. Start with the Devil directly to the Survivor's left. (First Devil \_ Survivor \_ Second Devil \_ Survivor \_ Third Devil \_ Survivor, and so on.)
- The Survivor never makes an opening ✶ call. If the Survivor wins a round, the next Devil to play opens the next hand.
- The Devil cannot issue a challenge ✶ against one of his other heads - only against the Survivor.
- If a Devil's head loses all their dice, ✶ that player cannot raise or call.


## GIVING UP DICE

Devils, each time you give up a die, reveal to the Survivor one grievance against them that you've always withheld. Make it mean.

Survivor, each time you give up a die, slip in one final, scathing little dig at the rest of the band. Make it petty.

## THE END

Survivor, if you knock out all of the other players' dice, you beat the Devil fair and square - for real this time. Describe what it looks like when you banish him for good.

If the Devil knocks out all of your remaining dice, he takes of you precisely what he came for. Name the last thing you see before you are claimed.

Going around the table, name what still remains in this freight car.

After this, the game is over.


Restless night.

## NOTES &amp; EPHEMERA

Miscellaneous notes, edge cases, and other such sundries.

## ON THE FINAL CONFRONTATION

In the event that the Survivor enters the Final Confrontation with only one or two dice in their spoils, you should still round up to three so that every player gets to contribute during the finale.

## ON MAXING OUT

If a player ever wagers the highest possible dice limit (e.g., if there are only three dice left, and a player wagers three sixes), the next call issued must be a challenge.


## ON VIRTUAL &amp; ONLINE PLAY

Liar's Dice is a game that is best suited for in-person play, but it's possible to play online. One easy way is to have each player roll individually, using a dice-rolling program of their choosing, and have everyone share a screenshot during the final count.

You can also play Liar's Dice using a virtual tabletop (VTT), where all players can see and interact with each other's dice. If you do so, it's important to make sure that each player can keep their dice concealed until the final count.

## A STORYTELLING GAME of DICE, DEVILS, and POOR DECISIONS

Thirteen years ago, four musicians struck a deal with the Devil for fame, riches, and stardom. Now they're fleeing their cursed contract, hiding in a boxcar on  a  train  to  who-knows-where,  with only one question between them: Whose fault is it that we wound up in this mess?

Don the roles of these messy bandmates in a high-stakes dice game of bluffing, secrets, and betrayal. Build your quartet, tell the story of their rise and inevitable fall,  and  reflect  together  on  all  the choices  you  made  that  led  you  down this sorry path.
